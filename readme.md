To simulate the dynamics of power systems and assess system reliability, as well as quantifying component importance, scholars and practitioners have adopted DC (direct current) flow models. These are a relaxation of the AC (alternating current) model required for power balance equations and electrical engineering constraints to simulate the dynamics of the grid. Due to their linear characteristics, DC flow models are computationally efficient and are widely used (and attractive for probability studies). However, DC flow models sometimes fail to approximate actual cascading processes due to violations of many assumptions made by DC models. For example, they neglect the quadratic equation of reactive power balance, assume all voltage magnitudes are equal to 1 per unit, ignore line losses, and overlook tap-dependent impedance in transformers, among others. Therefore, efforts have been made to move towards AC-based models [6]. Nedic et al. [7] validated and examined the criticality of 1,000 bus networks using an AC outage model, which represents many interactions in cascading faults. However, this model does not account for intergeneration adjustments. Consequently, Mei et al. [8] proposed a new method for studying cascading failures and outages in power systems based on AC-OPF and self-organizing criticality theory, taking into account both fast and slow dynamic processes. As an alternative, focusing on cascading processes, AC flow models and AC optimal power flow considerations were employed to propose an AC-based model to simulate cascading failures in power systems, better informing resource prioritization. This paper presents an AC-based cascading failure approach that relies not only on more accurate power flow assessments but also corrects important phenomena inherent to DC flow analysis, such as overestimation of high load node importance by DC-based models and underestimation of low load nodes.
Through the calculation of power system dynamics, it is necessary to have a method based on performance degradation to measure the importance of each component. Since the early work on importance measures in the 1960s [9], various importance measures (IM) have been studied to determine the relative strength of components in the system according to different criteria. Among them, topological importance measures are primarily derived from network science and are one of the most widely used measures, including degree centrality, closeness centrality [10], betweenness centrality [11], information centrality [12], PageRank [13], hypertext induced topic selection algorithm (HITS) [14]. In addition to topological IM, structural importance measures [15], lifespan importance measures [2], resilience importance measures [3], [4], risk importance measures [16], [17] and reliability importance measures [18], [19] are also applicable to different disciplines.
Examples of risk importance metrics include the Risk Reduction (RR) measures, Risk Reduction Value (RRW) measures, Fussel-Vesely (FV) Importance Index [20], Risk Achievement (RA) indicator, Risk Achievement Value (RAW) indicator, and Birnbaum Importance (BI) and Partial Derivative (PD) measures [21]. Some of these importance measurement standards can easily be converted into performance-based importance measures. For example, RA, RAW, and FV are calculated using P(base)-P(i=0), P(base)/P(i=0), and [P(base)-P(i=0)]/P(base), where P(base) is the performance of the original system and P(i=0) is the system performance when an element i has been removed from the system. These measures are applicable to most schemes but not all. For instance, power systems are typically managed to meet N-1 contingencies, meaning that any failure of a component does not result in any performance degradation except for the loss of the component itself, thus making it impossible to distinguish the importance levels of components. Additionally, events are often caused by combinations of faults. To address this, this paper proposes an AC-based flow element importance metric that considers multiple components in the system.
The proposed element importance metric not only minimizes the ranking relationship but also applies an AC-based cascading fault model to capture unique phenomena in power systems. Using the AC-based cascading model, the flow can better reflect system performance and reliability, allowing for more accurate assessments of component importance. Therefore, the recommended importance metric can assist decision-makers and power grid companies in identifying key elements within the system and more effectively implementing reliability improvements.
